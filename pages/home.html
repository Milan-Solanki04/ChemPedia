Reading the table

The table's shape is not a convenience. A row and a column each mean one thing, and once you can see what they mean, most of the chemistry can be read off the page.

Periods

Across a row

A period is every element between two successive closed shells, and it runs from the first element to add an electron to a new shell to the last. Elements in one period differ by one proton, so the row fills left to right while the number of electrons in the outermost shell stays the same. That is why an element's radius barely changes across its row, while how strongly it pulls on shared electrons changes a great deal.

Groups

Down a column

A group is a column, and the elements in it share the same arrangement of electrons in their outermost shell. Almost all of a group's chemistry is the chemistry of that one shell: which elements they are, and how far each is from filling it. Going down a group adds shells without adding a position in the outer one, so the elements get larger and hold on to their outer electrons less tightly.
